AI Value & Readiness Workbook

By Jengara · Practical working guide

An AI opportunity needs a defined business outcome, evidence that delivery is feasible and an accountable next decision. This workbook brings those elements together before a team commits to a pilot. Complete one copy for one opportunity, with a business owner, delivery lead and the people responsible for data and controls. Treat unknowns as work to resolve. The workbook supports a discussion; it does not certify readiness.

1. Frame the decision before choosing technology

Name the process and the customer or colleague it serves. Describe the decision you need to make: whether to investigate, pilot, defer or stop. Record the executive sponsor, process owner and technical lead so that responsibility extends beyond the working session. Choose a primary outcome and capture the current baseline with its source.

Map today's workflow from its trigger through inputs, systems, decision points and human effort. Then describe the proposed AI intervention. Be explicit about what remains with a person and which actions must never happen automatically. A useful scope makes both the intended improvement and the operating boundary visible.

  • What happens today, and where is the measurable problem?
  • What would count as an accepted result?
  • Who owns the measure, and over what observation period?

2. Build a readiness evidence log

Work through six areas: data, workflow, integration, people, controls and evaluation. Mark each as evidenced, partial or unknown. Attach a source, an accountable owner and a next action. The purpose is to expose the constraint that could change the decision, rather than produce a reassuring aggregate score.

For data, check permissions, usability and maintenance. For workflow, inspect exceptions and hand-offs. For integration, establish whether the required interfaces exist. Identify who accepts outputs and owns adoption. Describe access, oversight and recovery arrangements, then specify the evaluation that will demonstrate acceptable output.

Select the most consequential evidence gap. Write down how you will resolve it and what decision could change when the evidence arrives. If there is no reliable baseline, make measuring it the first deliverable.

3. Make the value case reviewable

Record eligible monthly volume, current cost or time per unit, expected accepted-outcome rate and the proposed change in value or cost per outcome. Include build, integration and change costs alongside recurring technology and oversight costs. Keep estimates separate from measured observations and use ranges where evidence is weak.

Test lower adoption, additional review effort and more retries. Ask which assumption would remove the business case and how a pilot could test it. Avoid counting the same benefit twice: time saved does not automatically become payroll reduction without a separate plan to realize that saving.

4. Set evidence gates and a bounded next step

Record your decision and its rationale. A pilot needs a named owner, a spending limit and a stop condition. Organize the next steps around evidence gates rather than activity alone:

  1. Before pilot: establish permitted data and a safe scope.
  2. Before release: demonstrate quality, oversight and recovery.
  3. Before scale: evidence realized value and an operating owner.

Use the 30-, 60- and 90-day fields to assign the next three deliverables. Each should have an accountable person and a clear completion condition. Preserve the source evidence and assumptions with the completed workbook, and revisit the decision when workload, data, architecture or business conditions change.

The downloadable workbook provides writing space for each stage. Use this guide to prepare the session, then retain the completed pages as a record of what is known, what remains uncertain and who will obtain the next evidence.
