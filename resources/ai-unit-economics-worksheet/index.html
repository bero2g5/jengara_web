AI Unit Economics Worksheet

By Jengara · Practical working guide

The unit that matters is a successful business outcome. This worksheet helps engineering, product and finance agree what success means, count the attributable operating cost and test the assumptions that change the result. Use one workload and one measurement period per worksheet. The downloadable version contains formulas and blank inputs; it does not calculate automatically.

1. Define the business unit and acceptance criteria

Choose a unit such as a resolved support case, approved claim or accepted design. State the quality, latency and control conditions that must all be met for that unit to count as accepted. Record the period, currency, workload segment, source records and finance and technical owners.

Keep accepted business outcomes separate from API calls, attempts and technical completions. An output that completed technically may still fail the business acceptance criteria. Agree these definitions before comparing models or vendors so that the comparison uses a consistent denominator.

2. Draw the full operating cost boundary

Count costs attributable to the workload, including failed and abandoned attempts. The worksheet covers model inference and embeddings; retrieval, storage and data movement; orchestration and external tools; evaluation, observability and controls; compute capacity; human review and exception handling; and platform operations and support.

For every category, record the period cost and its allocation method or source. State whether shared costs are assigned by usage, capacity, customer, time or another defensible driver. Avoid allocating a shared cost twice. Reconcile the total to the agreed records and explicitly list excluded costs and why they are excluded.

Keep one-time investment visible separately. If you amortize it, state the assumption so that another reviewer can understand the comparison.

3. Build an outcome ledger

Record business units initiated, attempts including retries, technically completed units, accepted outcomes and human review hours. Use matching workload and period boundaries for every calculation:

  • Acceptance rate = accepted outcomes divided by business units initiated.
  • Attempts per accepted outcome = total attempts divided by accepted outcomes.
  • Operating cost per accepted outcome = total attributable period cost divided by accepted outcomes.
  • Review cost = review hours multiplied by fully loaded hourly cost.

If accepted outcomes are zero, cost per accepted outcome is undefined. Record the failure and its context. Do not replace the denominator with requests or tokens to make the number available. Preserve quality failures and exclusions alongside the calculated result.

4. Compare explicit scenarios

Enter a base case, downside case and improved case. Record monthly business volume, acceptance rate, attempts per outcome, variable and fixed operating costs, accepted outcomes and cost per accepted outcome. Treat scenarios as estimates until measured.

Explain each change: adoption, context size, retry behavior, model routing, review effort or vendor price. When volume changes, separate fixed from variable cost and identify capacity that grows in steps. Revalidate quality when changing models, caching or routing; a lower technical cost is useful only when the result still meets the agreed acceptance conditions.

5. Connect unit cost to realized value

Record revenue or avoided cost per accepted outcome, its evidence source and the mechanism that realizes the value. Build a bridge from attributable value to attributable cost, and state any additional costs needed to support a gross-margin claim. Keep the interpretation within the boundary you have measured.

Assign actions across engineering efficiency, product acceptance and adoption, and finance allocation and budget. Set success criteria, an exception threshold and a review date. The decision to scale should follow evidence of outcome quality, full cost and operational ownership. Revisit the worksheet when the workload changes so that the decision remains connected to current operating conditions.
