AI infrastructure is now critical infrastructure

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara Advisory · 26 August 2026 · Research and executive analysis

Production AI depends on a chain of digital and physical systems. Weakness in any link can become a constraint on deployment, reliability, margin or enterprise value.

Research approach

Jengara analysis combining current International Energy Agency projections with cloud, network and data-centre dependency analysis. Forecast figures are attributed to the IEA; the investment implications are Jengara’s interpretation.

Power is becoming a deployment constraint

The IEA projects global data-centre electricity consumption to reach roughly 945 TWh by 2030 in its base case, about twice the current level. Capacity strategy must therefore assess connection queues, grid constraints, generation mix and the time needed to deliver power—not only the price of compute.

AI density changes facility assumptions

Accelerated compute raises rack density, cooling demand and the consequences of local power or thermal limits. A portfolio plan based on conventional data-centre averages can underestimate both time-to-capacity and retrofit capital.

Networks determine usable compute

Accelerators create value only when data can reach them and distributed systems can exchange state within performance requirements. Interconnection, latency, congestion, egress economics and regional failure domains belong in the AI capacity model.

Cloud concentration is also infrastructure concentration

Using multiple model APIs does not automatically create resilience when they depend on the same cloud region, identity plane, data pipeline or network path. Dependency mapping should follow shared failure domains rather than vendor counts.

Cyber controls expand with agency

Agents add identities, tool permissions, memory, external actions and new data flows. Control design must cover the full execution chain and prove that failures can be detected, contained and investigated.

Boards need one evidence model

Cloud, network, data-centre, power, cyber and AI teams often report separately. A decision-grade resilience view connects capacity, service levels, concentration, recovery objectives, capital requirements and accountable owners.

Jengara investment implication

AI infrastructure should be assessed as critical infrastructure when its failure can interrupt material revenue, regulated processes, customer commitments or strategic growth. That threshold—not the novelty of the technology—should determine governance intensity.

Executive takeaway

The quality of an AI decision depends on linking technical evidence to economics, controls, operating capacity and accountable action.
