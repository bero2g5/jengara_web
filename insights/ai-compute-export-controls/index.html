The chip war is now your supply chain problem: export controls are redrawing the map of global AI compute

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara Advisory · 10 October 2026 · Research and executive analysis

The fastest growing risk in AI infrastructure is not the chip shortage. It is the map. Export controls, criminal enforcement and sovereign compute programs are splitting the global AI compute market into separate stacks, separate prices and separate rules. Any enterprise still treating GPU capacity as a neutral commodity is working from an old map.

Research approach

Original Jengara framework built on 2026 reporting and analysis from the US Bureau of Industry and Security, Reuters, Caixin, CSIS and industry outlets, plus Jengara analysis of enterprise supply chain exposure. Named figures are listed with their sources below. The model, structure and conclusions are our own.

The controls have moved from rules to enforcement

On January 13 2026 the Bureau of Industry and Security moved Nvidia H200 and AMD MI325X shipments to China and Macau from a presumption of denial to case by case review, with conditions that include a 25 percent tariff, a 50 percent volume cap measured against US customer volume, third party testing inside the United States, know your customer checks and exporter certification that shipments will not constrain US semiconductor supply. On May 31 2026 BIS closed the subsidiary loophole: exporting advanced computing items to an entity headquartered in, or with an ultimate parent headquartered in, Country Group D:5 or Macau now needs a license regardless of where the receiving subsidiary sits. The rule covers Nvidia Blackwell and Rubin and AMD MI350X, and it shut the channel through which Chinese firms bought chips through subsidiaries in Malaysia, Singapore and the UAE. Chips already installed are grandfathered.

Enforcement is criminal now, not just administrative. A Manhattan grand jury indictment unsealed on March 19 2026 charged the Supermicro co founder and two others with conspiring to route about 2.5 billion dollars in Nvidia equipped AI servers to China through a Southeast Asian shell company during 2024 and 2025, with defendants facing up to 30 years. Taiwan executed its first criminal AI chip smuggling crackdown on May 21 2026 and raided Supermicro Taiwan offices on June 29 2026. Across four public cases the reported figures total more than 10 billion dollars in scrutinized Nvidia linked hardware.

Action for leaders: audit your vendor chain for ultimate ownership, not just shipping address. Compliance has shifted from a destination question, did the chip go to China, to an ownership question, is the ultimate parent Chinese. Apply the same bar Nvidia now applies to its own buyers: ownership structure documentation and proof of actual data center capacity.

One license away from the cloud

The next frontier is not the chip in the box. It is the chip you rent. The Remote Access Security Act passed the House on January 12 2026 by 369 votes to 22 and would add remote access to US jurisdiction compute as a licensable activity, so a data center in Malaysia renting H100 access to a Chinese AI firm would need a BIS license. Senator Dave McCormick, a sponsor of the Senate companion, put it plainly: under current law, bad actors can train AI models by accessing advanced chips under the jurisdiction of the US, and the Bureau of Industry and Security has no authority to require a license. Separately, the Chip Security Act passed the House Foreign Affairs Committee 42 to 0 on March 26 2026 and would require location verification mechanisms on exported covered chips within 180 days of enactment, plus mandatory diversion reporting. Both bills are live but not yet enacted, and negotiators have agreed to fold them into the FY2027 defense authorization, where Senate Democrats are simultaneously blocking floor consideration over Iran war concerns.

Meanwhile the September 24 2026 Trump Xi summit in Washington produced an AI incident hotline and a recurring Super Intelligence Dialogue, but export controls were not on the AI agenda and Blackwell restrictions stayed intact. Compute policy is now a bargaining chip in statecraft, not a settled compliance regime.

Action for leaders: map your cloud tenancy to jurisdiction. If remote access licensing becomes law, renting compute abroad stops being a paperwork shortcut and becomes a controlled activity. Know which legal regime each of your GPU hours sits under before a regulator asks.

China is building its own stack anyway

Jensen Huang told CNBC in May 2026 that Nvidia has largely conceded the Chinese AI chip market to Huawei, and he has separately called the restrictions a failure that ceded share and accelerated Chinese domestic chip development. Bernstein projects Nvidia China AI chip share falling from about 40 percent in 2025 to about 8 percent by the end of 2026, with Huawei reaching about 50 percent and reportedly heading toward 12 billion dollars in annual AI chip revenue. The DeepSeek CEO has stated that chip bans force Chinese companies to use two to four times the computing power for equivalent results. The H20 license saga tells the story in miniature: licensed indefinitely in April 2025 at the cost of a 4.5 billion dollar quarterly charge, reversed that July, then sold again only under a 15 percent revenue share paid to the US government, while Nvidia fiscal 2026 guidance assumed zero data center compute revenue from China.

CSIS 2026 analysis calls the localization drive the controls principal effect: domestic procurement mandates, expanding fab capacity and coordinated innovation from AI accelerators to open instruction set architectures and lithography. CSIS senior adviser Charles Wessner writes that export controls have not stopped the Chinese advance but accelerated it, and once developers build on the Huawei stack, they do not switch back.

Action for leaders: plan for two stacks, not one. Any roadmap that assumes a single global hardware and software standard for the next five years is already dated. Ask every AI vendor where their software runs on non Nvidia accelerators and what the porting cost looks like.

Sovereign buyers are bidding up your hardware

The fragmentation is priced in. The EU has called for up to seven AI gigafactories with 10 billion euros in public support expected to unlock at least 20 billion euros in private investment, each site holding at least 100,000 advanced AI processors. France has committed 109 billion euros, with Mistral Compute deploying 18,000 Nvidia Grace Blackwell GPUs. Saudi Arabia is building a 40 billion dollar AI investment vehicle, with the HUMAIN program reportedly envisioning 11 data centers and 2.2 gigawatts of capacity. The UAE Stargate project targets a 1 gigawatt OpenAI cluster inside a planned 5 gigawatt campus, and G42 data center arm Khazna reports 650 megawatts operational. India subsidizes AI compute at 100 rupees per hour, about 42 percent below the global 2.5 to 3 dollar market rate, with more than 34,381 GPUs empanelled and a 100,000 GPU target for December 2026.

These programs buy into the same shortage you face. Nebius card rates in October 2026 show H100 at 3.85 to 4.50 dollars per GPU hour, up 16.9 percent, H200 at 4.50 to 5.40 dollars, up 20 percent, B200 at 7.15 to 8.50 dollars, up 18.9 percent, and B300 at 7.85 to 9.50 dollars, up 21 percent. Since May, H100 is up 53 percent and B300 up 56 percent. B200 residual value has hit 158 percent of launch, with street buyers paying more than 45,000 dollars against a roughly 30,000 dollar list, on a manufacturing cost estimate from Epoch AI of 5,700 to 7,300 dollars. HBM prices doubled in the first quarter of 2026. Microsoft has disclosed an 80 billion dollar Azure backlog it cannot fulfill because of power constraints. The five large hyperscalers are expected to spend more than 450 billion dollars in AI specific capital expenditure in 2026, about 1 percent of US GDP by the Goldman Sachs estimate. Neocloud instances run 60 to 70 percent cheaper than hyperscaler equivalents, and the hierarchy is inverting: hyperscalers are renting capacity from neoclouds.

Action for leaders: budget for price persistence, not price decline. Sovereign demand is a structural buyer that does not respond to normal demand signals. Lock base capacity where you can and stress test your AI unit economics at twice the current compute cost.

Jengara decision rule

Buy optionality, not chips. The winning position in a fragmented compute market is not the lowest price per GPU hour. It is a portfolio: contracted base capacity, portable software that runs on more than one accelerator, jurisdiction mapped deployments, and a vendor chain you can document under oath.

Executive takeaway

Export controls have turned AI compute from a commodity purchase into a geopolitical position. Enterprises that map their exposure now will buy capacity and optionality while both are still for sale.
