The power ceiling: energy is now the binding constraint on AI scale

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara Advisory · 6 October 2026 · Research and executive analysis

The limiting factor on AI scale has moved. It is no longer the chip. It is the megawatt.

Research approach

Jengara original analysis built on the IEA Energy and AI report of April 2026, Gartner and Axis Intelligence 2026 consumption estimates, grid operator auction and interconnection records, and public hyperscaler power agreements tracked through mid 2026. Where sources disagree, ranges are shown.

The power ceiling is the point at which AI expansion is limited not by compute supply, model capability or capital, but by physical access to electricity: generation, transmission, grid connections and the equipment that links a data center to the network.

Count the demand, then count the grid

Global data center electricity demand reached an estimated 565 terawatt hours in 2026, up about 26 percent from 2025, according to Gartner data compiled by Axis Intelligence. The IEA projects demand near 945 terawatt hours by 2030, roughly the total electricity consumption of Japan. AI focused facilities are the engine: their electricity use surged 50 percent in 2025 and is set to triple by 2030.

In the United States, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projections cited in 2026 place data center consumption between 325 and 580 terawatt hours by 2028, and the IEA expects US data centers to account for nearly half of all electricity demand growth through 2030. US data center power demand stood at 31 gigawatts in 2025 and is projected to reach 66 gigawatts in 2027, absorbing 8.5 percent of peak summer national demand.

Morgan Stanley projected a 15 gigawatt US power shortfall against expected AI chip deployments by 2027, widening to about 33 gigawatts by 2028, roughly six New York Cities, in a September 2026 update covered by the Financial Times. Only 50 to 60 percent of data center capacity scheduled for the next one to two years is expected to come online on time.

Follow the buyers: the nuclear pivot is real

Hyperscalers are not waiting for the grid. By May 2026, thirteen announced nuclear agreements totaled about 9.8 gigawatts across seven buyers, tracked by SMR Intel and deal researchers.

Microsoft holds the earliest delivery: an 835 megawatt twenty year agreement for the restart of Three Mile Island Unit 1, targeting first power in the second half of 2027. Amazon holds up to 1.92 gigawatts from Talen Energy's Susquehanna plant through 2042, plus a 700 million dollar stake in X energy's Xe 100 reactor program. Google committed 500 megawatts from Kairos Power with the first unit targeted for 2030. Meta leads on volume with up to 6.6 gigawatts across TerraPower, Oklo, Vistra and Constellation, with deliveries targeted from 2030 to 2035.

Behind the meter gas generation and fuel cells fill the gap while nuclear remains years away. Power procurement is now a core AI infrastructure capability.

Where the grid actually breaks

The deeper constraint is connection, not generation. Interconnection queues in hubs such as Northern Virginia, Phoenix and Dallas routinely stretch four to seven years. Large power transformer deliveries stretch to two years or more, according to the Department of Energy, while gas turbine deliveries run three to five years, according to Wood Mackenzie.

PJM Interconnection's capacity auction for the 2027 and 2028 delivery year cleared at the price cap of 333.44 dollars per megawatt day, with total capacity costs of 16.1 billion dollars, up from 2.2 billion three years earlier. About 97 percent of the load growth came from data centers. On 29 September 2026, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission suspended PJM's emergency backstop procurement of 6.8 gigawatts until February 2027 over cost allocation and data center demand concerns.

A growing coalition argues large technology companies should fund their own grid upgrades rather than shifting costs onto residential ratepayers, whose prices have risen 42 percent since 2019. Who pays will be litigated market by market, shaping project economics and pricing.

The efficiency paradox

Individual AI tasks are getting dramatically more efficient. The IEA's April 2026 report compiled the first official per query figures: a median Gemini text prompt at 0.24 watt hours and an average ChatGPT query at 0.34 watt hours.

The catch is workload mix. A medium sized model uses about 0.05 watt hours of GPU electricity for a text response; a reasoning model uses about 1.14. An agentic task with reasoning and four to six model calls uses about 50 watt hours, roughly a thousand times the basic response. GPU racks draw 40 to 80 kilowatts or more, against 5 to 10 for standard racks. Per task efficiency gains are real, and the shift to reasoning, agents and video is overwhelming them.

What executives and investors should do now

First, make power the first diligence question on every AI infrastructure decision. Site selection begins with grid interconnection, not real estate.

Second, demand the power plan behind every capacity announcement: contracted supply, queue position, equipment lead times. A June 2026 Carnegie Endowment analysis argues hyperscaler nuclear commitments exceed what US supply chains can deliver on schedule. Only restarts and operating plant agreements deliver power before 2030.

Third, build schedule contingency into AI roadmaps, since only about half of planned capacity arrives on time, and track the cost allocation fight market by market.

Jengara decision rule

Approve AI capacity plans only against secured and deliverable power. Treat announced gigawatts as options, and keep a power contingency on every roadmap.

Executive takeaway

Electricity access is now the scarcest input in AI. The winners of the next buildout phase will be the ones that secured power before they announced compute.
