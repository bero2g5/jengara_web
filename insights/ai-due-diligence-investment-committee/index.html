Seven AI diligence questions every investment committee should ask

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara Advisory · 26 August 2026 · Research and executive analysis

AI diligence should establish whether a claimed capability is technically defensible, commercially sustainable and governable at scale—not merely whether a compelling demonstration exists.

Research approach

Jengara synthesis based on established AI risk-management guidance, European regulation and infrastructure operating practice. The seven questions below are Jengara’s original investment-decision framework.

1. Is AI material to the investment thesis?

Separate core product capability from optional feature narrative. Identify the revenue, retention, margin or operating-leverage mechanism and define the evidence required to validate it. A technically impressive feature that does not change customer behaviour or economics should not carry material valuation weight.

2. Is the data advantage durable and permissible?

Map the provenance, permissions, quality, refresh cycle and substitutability of the data used in training, retrieval and evaluation. The key diligence question is not simply whether data exists, but whether the company can continue using it and whether competitors can reproduce the advantage.

3. Can the architecture operate at forecast scale?

Test model dependencies, orchestration, retrieval, evaluation, observability, latency and failure recovery against management’s growth assumptions. Production readiness should be evidenced through measured service levels and failure modes rather than architecture diagrams alone.

4. Do the economics survive adoption?

Model the fully loaded cost per successful outcome. Include input and output tokens, retries, retrieval, tool calls, guardrails, human review, accelerator capacity and platform operations. Run sensitivity cases for usage growth, vendor pricing and quality thresholds.

5. Is concentration risk understood?

Identify dependencies on model vendors, clouds, specialist staff, proprietary data and geographic capacity. Evaluate portability in practical terms: time, cost, quality loss and operational risk—not whether an alternative exists in theory.

6. Are controls proportionate to the use case?

NIST’s Generative AI Profile frames risk management across the AI lifecycle, while the EU AI Act applies obligations according to role and risk. Diligence should therefore test inventory, accountability, evaluation, access, monitoring, incident response and human oversight in the context of the actual use case.

7. What must happen after close?

Translate every material finding into an owner, cost range, evidence gate and timing. Separate deal protections from first-100-day work and longer-term platform investment so the investment committee can see downside exposure and value-creation potential.

Executive takeaway

The quality of an AI decision depends on linking technical evidence to economics, controls, operating capacity and accountable action.
